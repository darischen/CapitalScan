The stop width — the finding that matters

A second sweep varied stop_atr_k, which the grid above holds fixed at 1.5. It is the dominant exit parameter: 9 bp from tightest to none, against about 1 bp for the target. All at a 5% target, train first.

Monotonic on both splits, no turnover. Target hits barely move across the whole range (19.2% to 23.7%) while stop-outs go 55% to 0% and become timeouts — the stop was not protecting winners, it was converting recoverable trades into realised losses.

The control wins and is still not the recommendation. No stop is the only one of fourteen arms positive on train, but it costs 11 points of worst case (−39.55% to −50.34%) and leaves 72.6% of trades exiting on the five-day timeout, which makes max_hold_days the real policy — a parameter never swept. k=2.0 takes most of the gain, keeps a real stop, and its worst trade is identical to k=1.5's: the same trade gapped through both.

What the sweep is measured on, and what it is not

Selection is on net_ret, not on the FDR q-values the cell statistics produce. hit_flags derives p_hit from fwd_ret_5d — a fixed-window forward return, which is a fact about the market and carries no dependence on the exit policy. The first completed arm demonstrated it: mean forward return was identical to five decimals across both arms while net return moved and the stop-out rate went from 33% to 54%.

So zero cells surviving FDR in every arm is expected here and is not a result about the exit policy. The responsive measures are net return, the exit-reason mix, and the benchmark comparison against each arm's own null.
